1

How to build an apparatus

Every experiment completes an unfinished apparatus: a telescope needs its objective, an orrery its drive wheel, a battery its last cell. Transport the softly glowing missing part wholly into the marked assembly bay and hold it there for two seconds. The mounting jig then releases your carrier, seats the actual part in its socket and completes the apparatus.

Pick a part from the drawer and draw it inside the scribed build area: drag from joint to joint for rods, oak, string and springs; tap to drop a wheel or a counterweight; drag in the direction a piston should push. A tap that does not drag does not lay a rod. Ends that land on a joint are pinned there and turn freely. Each wheel also has four rim pins: a beam attached there follows the turning wheel like a crank. Pulley cord: tap one end, then up to four wheel hubs, then the other end. Winch cord: tap a wheel hub, then the other end. While either cord is open, the footer states how many ends are set. Rest on a part for its numbers: hover on a desk, press and hold on a phone.

Missing components carry a brass transport joint: pin your carrier to it, or push the component. Then pull the brass lever; pull it again to stop and edit.

Fantastic Apparatus was inspired by Colin Northway's Fantastic Contraption (2008), rebuilt with real masses and materials.

In the game

Only parts sharing the same pin do not collide with each other. Steel and wheels otherwise collide, even when indirectly joined in one frame. Oak is ghost to components and parts but still touches furniture. Assembly bays are marked activation volumes, not invisible collision walls; their rear-plane jigs seat the delivered parts automatically, not a simulation of screws or electrical circuits.

cf. I. Newton, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687): the laws of motion the simulation follows, with the modelling notes listed on the simplifications page. Masses and materials: the parts and strength pages.

2

The laboratory dials

Three brass dials watch every run: total mass of the parts you placed (kilograms), the work the motors and pistons put in (joules), and the elapsed time (seconds). The needles climb as the apparatus runs.

Each reading has a score S(x) = K / (a + x)^p (separate K, a, p for mass, energy and time; see economy-config). Beating your best pays the rise in score, round(S(new) − S(old)). Because S steepens as x approaches nought, the last gram, joule or tenth of a second is worth more than the first crude cut. A first clear still pays a flat bonus on top.

The laboratory remembers your best mass, best energy and best time on each experiment. Beat any of the three and the win card congratulates you by name ("New best mass", and so on).

In the game

Mass counts only the parts you placed (not the furniture or the specimens). Energy is the absolute mechanical work ∫|τω|dt of every motor joint plus ∫|F·v|dt of every piston over the run. A repeat clear with no new best pays only the small repeat purse; improvements pay ΔS as above. The dial full-scales are soft pegs for the needles (20 kg, 80 J, 30 s), not hard caps on the score.

cf. Work and energy, any elementary mechanics; purse rule from Fantastic Apparatus economy-config (owner, 2026-11-04).

The parts, with their real numbers

Spiked brass wheel: a solid 12 mm brass disc, 18 cm across, 2.6 kg, unpowered. Motor wheels (↻ and ↺): the same size, spoked rim ≈1.3 kg plus motor/housing ≈2.7 kg (4 kg), 4 N·m stall torque and 7 rad/s free speed (0.63 m/s at the rim).

Extras: oak beam (touches only furniture), coil spring (k = 300 N/m) and a 9 cm cast-iron ball of 2.7 kg.

In the game

The world is a flat slice: each part has the mass of the real object, spread over its 2D outline.

4

Levers and torque

A force turns things about a pivot in proportion to how far from the pivot it acts: the torque is τ = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force.

So a lever balances when F₁·d₁ = F₂·d₂: a small force far out holds a big force close in. Archimedes stated the law of the lever in On the Equilibrium of Planes.

A rod pinned to a wheel off-centre is a lever too: a push on its far end turns the wheel harder than the same push near the hub.

In the game

Rods and beams are perfectly rigid and never bend or break, however hard a piston leans on them.

cf. Archimedes, On the Equilibrium of Planes, Book I, Props. 6–7; D. Halliday, R. Resnick & J. Walker, Fundamentals of Physics, ch. 10–12.

5

Wheels and rolling

A wheel that rolls without slipping moves its centre at v = ω·r, and the point touching the ground is momentarily at rest. That is why rolling costs so little: the contact does not rub.

A rolling wheel carries energy in two forms, ½mv² for moving and ½Iω² for spinning. The moment of inertia I depends on where the mass sits: a uniform disc has I = ½mr², a thin hoop I = mr².

Down a slope of angle θ a rolling body accelerates at g·sin θ / (1 + I/mr²): a disc at ⅔ g·sin θ, a hoop at ½ g·sin θ. A spoked wheel keeps most of its mass in the rim, so it behaves more like a hoop: slower to spin up, slower to stop.

In the game

Every wheel, the spoked motor wheels included, is simulated as a uniform disc (I = ½mr²), and there is no rolling resistance at all.

cf. Halliday, Resnick & Walker, Fundamentals of Physics, ch. 11 (rolling); J. R. Taylor, Classical Mechanics (2005), ch. 10.

6

Friction, and why spikes grip

Dry friction follows Coulomb's law: a resting block will not slide until the sideways force exceeds μₛN (N is the force pressing the surfaces together), and once it slides the friction is about μₖN, a little less. To a first approximation it does not depend on the area in contact (Amontons, 1699).

Typical textbook values (they vary a lot with finish and dirt): steel on steel μₛ ≈ 0.74, μₖ ≈ 0.57; wood on wood μₛ ≈ 0.25–0.5; glass on glass μₛ ≈ 0.9 but μₖ ≈ 0.4.

Spikes go beyond plain Coulomb friction: they dig into the surface, so the wheel must plough or shear material to slip. The grip then behaves like a friction coefficient above 1.

In the game

One coefficient serves for both static and sliding friction, and two touching materials combine as √(μ₁μ₂) (the planck.js rule). Spikes are just a high coefficient (1.1); nothing is dented.

cf. C.-A. Coulomb, Théorie des machines simples (1781 prize essay; publ. 1785); R. A. Serway & J. W. Jewett, Physics for Scientists and Engineers, Table 5.1; F. P. Bowden & D. Tabor, The Friction and Lubrication of Solids (1950).

7

String tension

A string can only pull. Taut, it carries the same tension T all along its length (if it is light and runs over nothing rubbing), and each end pulls along its adjoining straight span. A frictionless guide redirects the pull without changing the tension.

Slack, it carries nothing at all. That makes strings ideal for tethers, for catching things that swing too far, and for dragging loads that need room to move.

Rope makers' tables give a minimum breaking force of 1.8 kN for new 5 mm (3/16 in) manila. Scaled by cross-section, the 2 mm cord in the drawer holds about 320 N, and the game snaps it at 300 N: twice the piston's push, but a cord holding a heavy swinging load can reach it.

In the game

The string is massless and does not stretch: it is a tension-only maximum-length constraint and snaps at 300 N. The drawn sag is illustrative. Pulley cord routes one continuous length through up to four wheel hubs, treated as frictionless zero-radius eyes; the guides take the reaction force and can move. Winch cord winds on a wheel-radius drum with a tangent lead and opposing load torque. The drum has a constant radius and ideal unlimited payout; real cord thickness, groove friction and layering are not modelled.

cf. Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 5 (tension); Cordage Institute minimum breaking strengths for 3-strand manila rope (3/16 in: 405 lbf, 1.8 kN; 1/4 in: 540 lbf, 2.4 kN), as reprinted in rope makers' tables; planck.js RopeJoint.

8

Springs and stored energy

Stretch or squash a spring by x from its natural length and it pushes back with F = −k·x: Hooke's law, which Robert Hooke published in 1678 as "ut tensio, sic vis" (as the extension, so the force).

The work you do is stored as elastic energy ½k·x². This game's spring has k = 300 N/m: stretched 10 cm it pulls with 30 N and holds 1.5 J.

Real springs obey Hooke only up to their elastic limit; beyond it they stay bent.

In the game

Springs are massless, never overstretch, and are damped (4 N·s/m) so they settle instead of ringing for ever. They pass through everything.

cf. R. Hooke, Lectures de Potentia Restitutiva, or of Spring (London, 1678); Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 7–8.

9

Steam power: pressure × area

Steam pushes on every square millimetre of a piston, so the force is pressure × area. This game's piston has a 25 mm bore and 3 bar of steam above atmosphere: 3×10⁵ Pa × π × (0.0125 m)² ≈ 147 N, about the weight of 15 kg.

Thomas Newcomen's engine of 1712 (near Dudley Castle) filled its cylinder with low-pressure steam and condensed it with a spray of water; the vacuum let the atmosphere push the piston down. James Watt's separate condenser (patented 1769) stopped the cylinder being chilled every stroke and cut the coal used by about three-quarters.

A double-acting cylinder takes steam on both sides in turn. On the return stroke the piston rod occupies part of the face, so steam presses only on the ring around it: 3×10⁵ Pa × π × (12.5² − 7²) mm² ≈ 101 N with this game's 14 mm rod. The game models this: every piston pulls back with 101 N.

In a reciprocating engine the piston's to-and-fro drives a crank that turns a flywheel, as in the working engine in the catalogue.

A steam engine also times itself: an eccentric on the crankshaft slides the valve that sends steam to one side of the piston, then the other.

In the game

The steam never runs out or cools, and the pressure never sags; the piston pushes with a steady 147 N and pulls with 101 N, at up to 0.3 m/s either way. Every piston is timed by a plain clock (out for 1 s, back for 1 s), not by a valve worked from a crank.

cf. L. T. C. Rolt & J. S. Allen, The Steam Engine of Thomas Newcomen (1977); J. Watt, English patent No. 913 (1769); R. L. Hills, Power from Steam (1989); W. J. M. Rankine, A Manual of the Steam Engine and Other Prime Movers (1859), on slide valves.

10

Motors: torque against speed

A simple DC electric motor gives its greatest torque when held still (the stall torque) and none at its free-running speed; in between the torque falls off in a straight line.

Power is torque × speed, so it peaks at half the free speed: for a 4 N·m, 7 rad/s motor that would be 4 × 7 / 4 = 7 W, about a hundredth of a horsepower.

At the Vienna exhibition of 1873, a Gramme dynamo wired to another is said to have spun the second as a motor: the first big demonstration that dynamos could deliver power over a wire.

In the game

It depends how the wheel is mounted. A motor wheel pinned to a frame is a planck.js joint motor: full 4 N·m at any speed up to 7 rad/s, then it holds that speed instead of tapering off; the frame takes the equal-and-opposite reaction. A lone motor wheel carries a stator of 1.2 kg whose centre of mass sits 3 cm off the axle: the joint motor twists rotor and stator opposite ways, so Newton's third law holds. On the bench the sustained crawl torque is only about m·g·d = 0.35 N·m from the offset weight, plus 0.05 N·m of bearing friction — a few newtons at the rim, not the full stall. Either way the motor never overheats when stalled.

cf. A. E. Fitzgerald, C. Kingsley & S. D. Umans, Electric Machinery (DC machines); B. Bowers, A History of Electric Light and Power (1982), on Gramme and Fontaine.

11

Energy: kept, and lost

Energy is never made or destroyed, only moved about: a motor's work becomes the motion of your apparatus, height gained becomes potential energy mgh, and a falling weight turns it back into motion.

Every collision, every rub and every damped spring turns some of it into heat and sound. James Joule measured the exchange rate in the 1840s by stirring water with falling weights: about 4.2 J of work warms a gram of water by one degree.

Lifting a 4 kg weight 30 cm needs at least 4 × 9.81 × 0.3 ≈ 12 J; an apparatus that does it spends far more, mostly in friction and bumps.

In the game

The game does not track heat: lost energy simply disappears. It is lost to bounces (restitution below 1), friction, the springs' damping (4 N·s/m), a touch of angular damping on wheels and the counterweight (nothing else has any), and the integrator itself.

cf. J. P. Joule, "On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. 140, 61 (1850); Feynman, Lectures on Physics I, ch. 4.

12

Momentum, collisions and restitution

In a collision the total momentum, mass × velocity, is unchanged: whatever one body gains, the other loses.

How bouncy the impact is is set by the coefficient of restitution e, the speed of separation over the speed of approach (Newton's experimental law, from his pendulum experiments in the Principia). e = 1 is a perfect bounce; e = 0 means the two stick together.

A heavy cart nudging a 1 kg crystal sphere hands it momentum; with low e the sphere rolls off gently instead of shooting away.

In the game

Restitution values here are low (brass 0.15, rock crystal 0.25), and a pair uses the larger of the two (the planck.js rule) rather than a measured value for that pair.

cf. I. Newton, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), Scholium to the Laws of Motion; Taylor, Classical Mechanics, ch. 3.

13

Centre of mass and tipping

An object on a flat surface stands as long as the vertical line through its centre of mass falls inside its base. Lean it past that edge and gravity turns it over.

Push a block of width b sideways at height h and it tips rather than slides if h > b / (2μ): push low to slide a tea tin, high to topple it.

Tall, narrow, top-heavy things (a retort stand, an empty canister on end) tip easily; low, wide ones slide. A cast-iron counterweight slung low on a cart lowers its centre of mass and keeps it upright on a slope.

In the game

Every object is rigid, so it pivots exactly on its corner; real ones dent and rock a little.

cf. J. L. Meriam & L. G. Kraige, Engineering Mechanics: Statics, ch. 6 (tipping and sliding).

Margin note: see this for yourself in Fantastic Apparatus.

14

Inclined planes

On a slope of angle θ, gravity's pull mg splits into mg·sin θ down the slope and mg·cos θ into it. A ramp lets a weak motor lift a heavy load by pushing it a long way up a gentle slope instead of straight up.

The work is the same either way (force × distance = mg × height); the slope only trades force for distance. That trade also reduces the grip: the normal force mg·cos θ, and so the friction, falls as the slope steepens.

Simon Stevin proved the law of the inclined plane around 1586 with his "wreath of spheres", a loop of balls hung over a triangular prism that cannot move by itself.

In the game

The planks are perfectly flat and never flex under a cart.

cf. S. Stevin, De Beghinselen der Weeghconst (1586); Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 5–6.

15

Gravity on Earth, Mars and the Moon

Surface gravity is about 9.81 m/s² on Earth, 3.71 m/s² on Mars and 1.62 m/s² on the Moon. A 2 kg brass weight keeps its mass everywhere, but weighs 19.6 N here, 7.4 N on Mars and 3.2 N on the Moon.

Less weight means less normal force and so less friction: wheels slip more easily, and a cart that climbs a ramp on Earth may spin its wheels on the Moon. Things also fall more slowly: dropping 1 m takes 0.45 s on Earth and 1.1 s on the Moon (t = √(2h/g)).

Momentum and inertia do not change: a heavy cart is just as hard to start and stop on the Moon.

In the game

Only g changes. The air, temperature and the materials' friction coefficients stay as on Earth.

cf. NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Planetary Fact Sheets (Earth, Mars, Moon).

16

Materials and densities

Densities used for every part and specimen, in kg/m³: brass 8500, steel 7850, cast iron 7200, glass 2500, rock crystal 2650, brick 1900, oak 750.

Real values spread: brass alloys run about 8,400–8,750; grey cast iron about 7,000–7,300; seasoned oak anywhere from about 600 to 900 depending on the species and how dry it is.

Steel is the stiff, strong choice for rods; oak is light (a tenth of steel's density) for beams that need only to reach; brass and iron make heavy wheels and counterweights.

In the game

Each body is a 2D outline whose areal density is chosen so its total mass equals that of the real 3D object.

cf. W. M. Haynes (ed.), CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics; USDA Forest Products Laboratory, Wood Handbook, FPL-GTR-190 (2010), ch. 4–5.

17

How the simulation works

The game uses planck.js, a JavaScript rewrite of Erin Catto's Box2D rigid-body engine. It advances the world in fixed steps of 1/120 s (about 8 ms): first every body's velocity, from forces and gravity, then its position.

Joints and contacts are constraints ("these two points stay together", "these bodies do not overlap"). Box2D solves them by sequential impulses: it nudges the velocities of each pair in turn, sweeping over all of them several times per step (10 times here for velocities and 4 for positions) until they nearly agree.

A fixed step makes the result repeatable: the same design on the same level always does the same thing, which is what lets the tests replay every reference solution.

In the game

A constraint is only satisfied approximately, so pins can stretch by a few millimetres under load, and a fast part could in principle pass through a thin moving object between two steps (planck.js's continuous collision guards only the fixed furniture unless a body is marked as a bullet).

cf. E. Catto, "Iterative Dynamics with Temporal Coherence", Game Developers Conference (2005); Box2D v2 manual (recommended 8 velocity / 3 position iterations at 60 Hz); planck.js (github.com/piqnt/planck.js).

18

Pins, joints and constraints

A pin (revolute joint) keeps two points together but lets the parts turn freely about it. A piston is a sliding (prismatic) joint between cylinder and rod with a motor along it. A string is a maximum-distance rule.

Three rods pinned in a triangle cannot change shape, which is why bridges and cranes are made of triangles; four rods in a square fold flat.

Each joint removes freedoms: a flat body has three (x, y and rotation), and a pin takes two of them away.

In the game

Pins are frictionless. Each is a 1/8 in brass screw that shears at 969 N (see "Stress, strength and breaking"). A joint's constraint is solved approximately each step, so a heavily loaded pin may drift by a few millimetres.

cf. Meriam & Kraige, Engineering Mechanics: Statics, ch. 4 (trusses); Box2D v2 manual, "Joints".

19

The Leyden jar and the Wimshurst machine

A Leyden jar is a glass jar coated with metal foil inside and out: the earliest capacitor, found independently by Ewald von Kleist (1745) and Pieter van Musschenbroek at Leiden (1746). It stores electric charge on the two foils, separated by the glass.

James Wimshurst built his influence machine in the early 1880s: two glass discs turning in opposite directions carry charge to collecting combs, and a pair of Leyden jars stores it until a spark jumps the gap.

A Wimshurst machine is therefore about a decade younger than these notes, which are dated 1873; the catalogue offers it all the same.

In the game

They are decorations: no electricity is simulated, and a Leyden jar on the bench is only an obstacle of glass and brass.

cf. J. L. Heilbron, Electricity in the 17th and 18th Centuries (1979); S. P. Thompson, Elementary Lessons in Electricity and Magnetism (revised editions from the 1890s), on the Wimshurst machine.

20

Newton's three laws on the bench

First law: a body keeps its state of rest or steady motion unless a force acts on it. Second: force = mass × acceleration (F = ma), so the same push accelerates a 1 kg crystal ten times faster than a 10 kg load. Third: forces come in pairs; if A pushes B, B pushes A back just as hard, in the opposite direction.

The third law is why a piston needs something to brace against: as the rod pushes the specimen with 147 N, the cylinder is pushed back with 147 N, and your frame must carry it. A motor wheel on a cart twists the cart the other way as it turns.

Newton set the three laws out at the start of the Principia (1687).

In the game

The game keeps all three laws for pistons, motors, springs and strings: each force acts on both bodies. A lone motor wheel has a stator that takes the reaction torque; pinned motors push against their frame (see "Motors" and "Angular momentum").

cf. I. Newton, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), Axioms, or Laws of Motion; Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 5.

21

Spinning things keep spinning: angular momentum

A spinning body carries angular momentum L = Iω. With no outside torque it cannot change: that is why a free wheel coasts, and why a skater who pulls her arms in (smaller I) spins faster.

A motor wheel at its free speed has I = ½mr² ≈ 0.0162 kg·m², so L ≈ 0.113 kg·m²/s and a spin energy ½Iω² ≈ 0.4 J. A steam engine's heavy flywheel stores far more, which is what carries the crank smoothly past the dead points of each stroke.

When a motor spins a wheel one way, it twists its mounting (and so the cart) the other way, so the total angular momentum stays the same.

In the game

A lone motor wheel conserves angular momentum: the stator spins the other way with equal-and-opposite L. Pinned motors are honest too: the frame takes the reaction. One quieter leak remains: the small angular damping on the wheels and the counterweight is a twist with no partner, so it slowly drains L (a few thousandths of a kg·m²/s over a couple of seconds).

cf. Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 11 (angular momentum); Taylor, Classical Mechanics, ch. 3 and 10.

22

Springs that ring: damping and resonance

A mass m on a spring k bounces at its natural frequency f = (1/2π)√(k/m). This game's spring (k = 300 N/m) holding the 2.7 kg iron ball would bounce at about 1.66 Hz.

Damping (a force against the velocity, c·v) makes the bounce die away. The damping ratio is ζ = c / (2√(km)); for that ball ζ ≈ 0.07, so it rings for several bounces before it settles. ζ = 1 is critical damping: back to rest as fast as possible with no overshoot.

Push a lightly damped spring in time with its natural frequency and each push adds to the swing: resonance. The steady swing can reach about 1/(2ζ) times the static stretch, roughly 7 times here. A piston's 2 s cycle (0.5 Hz) is far from that ball's natural frequency, so it will not pump it up much.

In the game

Springs are massless and their damping (4 N·s/m) is a simple velocity force; real springs also lose energy inside the steel and can surge (ripple along their coils).

cf. A. P. French, Vibrations and Waves (1971), ch. 3–4; Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 15 (damped and forced oscillations).

23

Launching a specimen: projectile motion

Once a thing leaves your apparatus, only gravity acts on it (air drag is tiny for dense brass and glass at bench speeds). It keeps its sideways speed and gains downward speed at g, so it flies on a parabola: x = v·cos θ·t, y = v·sin θ·t − ½gt².

On level ground the range is R = v²·sin 2θ / g, longest at 45°. Thrown at 2 m/s at 45° it lands 0.41 m away on Earth and 2.5 m away on the Moon.

Galileo worked out the parabola in his Two New Sciences (1638) by treating the sideways and the falling motion separately.

In the game

There is no air at all, and the step-by-step integrator makes falls very slightly long (about 1 % over a 1 s fall at 120 steps per second).

cf. G. Galilei, Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze (1638), Fourth Day; Halliday, Resnick & Walker, ch. 4.

24

Stress, strength and breaking

Stress is force per area, σ = F/A. A 20 mm steel rod has 314 mm² of section. Mild steel yields at about 250 MPa, so at about 79 kN of pull the rod starts to stretch for good and narrows to a waist; it only parts at its ultimate strength, about 400 MPa, or 126 kN. A 30 × 15 mm oak beam has 450 mm²; clear oak pulled along the grain holds about 103 MPa, or 46 kN, and then snaps without stretching.

Pushed end-on, a slender member fails long before that by buckling: it bows sideways. Euler's formula for a pinned member gives P = π²EI/L², using the thinnest way it can bend. For a 1 m steel rod (E ≈ 200 GPa, I = πd⁴/64 = 7,854 mm⁴) that is 15.5 kN. For a 1.2 m oak beam bending the thin way (E ≈ 12.3 GPa, I = 30 × 15³/12 = 8,438 mm⁴) it is only about 710 N; at 0.6 m it is four times as much, 2.8 kN.

Euler overestimates short, stocky members, which yield or crush before they bow. Below a slenderness L/r of about 126 for this steel (rods shorter than 63 cm), and about 69 for oak (beams shorter than 30 cm), the game uses Johnson's parabola, P = Aσ(1 − σ(L/r)²/4π²E). That gives 54 kN for a 0.5 m rod, where Euler would claim 62 kN.

Bending is worst at the outer edge, the extreme fibre: σ = M·c/I + P/A. The steel rod yields at M = 250 MPa × 7,854 mm⁴ / 10 mm ≈ 196 N·m, about what a 1 m rod lying across a peg feels with 393 N hanging from each end. Steel is ductile: past yield it does not break but kinks, and the kink stays. After yielding, the elastic fibre formula no longer applies. A solid round bar has plastic section modulus Zp = d³/6, so full-plastic yield is 333 N·m and the model uses an ultimate bending capacity of 400 MPa × Zp = 533 N·m. The hinge hardens toward that capacity over an assumed 0.4 rad (23°); combined axial force reduces its bending allowance. This is an idealised capacity model, not a prediction of real fracture strain. Oak is brittle. Turned in its own plane the beam is 30 mm deep (I = 33,750 mm⁴), so it breaks outright at its modulus of rupture, 105 MPa, at 236 N·m. Pins carry no turning moment, so bending comes from things pressing along a member and from its own weight and inertia when it is struck.

Every joint is a 1/8 in brass screw, whose thread root (2.36 mm, 4.38 mm²) shears at 221 MPa, or 969 N. The 2 mm cord snaps at 300 N. So on this bench screws and cords give way long before any bar is pulled apart. Long beams buckle, steel rods kink, and a hard knock can bend or snap a member.

In the game

Parts fail as their material does. A steel rod that buckles or bends past yield kinks at that point and keeps the kink, still in one piece; pulled past yield it necks; it splits only at 400 MPa. An oak beam snaps where it fails, a screw shears and a cord snaps. Each has a quarter-second animation and sound. A part glows once it carries 70% of its failure load. The loads are read from the solver at every 1/120 s physics step and smoothed with a 6 ms time constant, so a one-step solver spike needs a third more than the failure load, while a genuine knock still breaks things. Members are otherwise perfectly rigid: the waist is drawn but the rod does not get longer, and a kink sits at least a fifth of the way along a rod.

cf. L. Euler, Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas (1744), appendix on elastic curves; J. M. Gere & B. J. How the apparatus is modelled

The game keeps real masses, sizes, densities, forces and gravity, but simplifies a few rules so that building stays playable.

Steel and wheels collide except at shared pins; oak passes through other parts but touches furniture; joints are frictionless; motors and pistons never run short of steam; the world is a flat 2D slice; wheels have no rolling resistance; air and heat are left out.

Parts can break, but members stay perfectly rigid until they do, and the breaking loads are those of new, flawless material. The solver's forces are smoothed over a step or two before they are compared with the breaking loads, because a stack of contacts can throw up a one-step spike that no real part would feel. The smoothing is a first-order lag with a 6 ms time constant, applied at every fixed 1/120 s physics step, so it is the same at any frame rate. A one-step spike must reach a third over the breaking load to count; a knock that lasts a few steps breaks at the true load.

A free motor wheel has a stator with an offset centre of mass: it can crawl on the bench with only a little force (about m·g·d at the axle, plus bearing friction), and the reaction torque never comes from nowhere.

Claims we softened or hedged after checking them:

  • Friction, and why spikes grip: Coefficients are quoted as "typical textbook values" (Serway Table 5.1) with a warning that they vary with finish; brass-on-steel was dropped because sources disagree widely.
  • Friction, and why spikes grip: Spikes: "behaves like a friction coefficient above 1" rather than a measured value; the ploughing mechanism is described qualitatively (Bowden & Tabor).
  • Motors: torque against speed: The 1873 Vienna Gramme/Fontaine story is told as "is said to have"; accounts of the exact circumstances vary. The game's motor is not claimed to be electric.
  • Steam power: pressure × area: Watt's coal saving "about three-quarters" is the commonly quoted figure for Watt vs Newcomen engines; individual comparisons varied.
  • String tension: The 2 mm cord's 300 N is scaled by cross-section from the Cordage Institute's 5 mm manila figure (1.8 kN), not measured on 2 mm cord. That is for new cord; knots, wet and age weaken it.
  • Motors: torque against speed: A free motor's sustained crawl is limited to about m·g·d from the stator's offset weight (plus a little bearing friction), not the full stall torque; the joint motor can still demand the stall briefly while the stator swings.
  • Energy: kept, and lost: Corrected after review: angular damping applies only to wheels and the counterweight, not every part.
  • Steam power: pressure × area: Corrected after review: the return-stroke force is now modelled (≈ 101 N on the annulus) instead of 147 N both ways.
  • Stress, strength and breaking: Steel yield "about 250 MPa" is for mild steel; Victorian wrought iron and steels varied. Oak values are the <i>Wood Handbook</i>'s for clear, dry white oak (E 12.3 GPa, MOR 105 MPa); real beams with knots or slope of grain are weaker.
  • Stress, strength and breaking: Oak's tension strength along the grain is not in the <i>Wood Handbook</i>'s Table 5-3. The game takes Table 5-7's overcup oak (a white oak), 77.9 MPa tested green, raised by the table's note of about 32% for hardwoods at 12% moisture: 103 MPa. That is a single species' average, and Table 5-6 gives tension a 25% coefficient of variation.
  • Stress, strength and breaking: Steel uses an idealised lumped hinge: it forms at first yield (196 N·m), representing the spread of plasticity, and hardens toward fu·Zp = 533 N·m with Zp = d³/6. Full-plastic yield is 333 N·m. The assumed 0.4 rad (23°) hardening rotation is not measured fracture strain. Parting uses the conservative linear capacity interaction |M|/(fu·Zp) + |N|/(fu·A), not elastic fibre stress after yield. Uniform section stress at UTS is a simplified envelope, not a calibrated ductile-damage law. Rods do not get longer when they neck.
  • Stress, strength and breaking: The 1/8 in brass screw is assumed to shear across its thread root; a screw with its plain shank (3.18 mm) in the joint would hold about 80% more.
  • How the apparatus is modelled: Breaking loads are for new, flawless material, and the spike smoothing means a very short knock must be a third over the load. Real parts break at notches, threads and flaws, often lower and less predictably.
  • Spinning things keep spinning: angular momentum: Free motors conserve angular momentum between rotor and stator; the small angular damping on the wheels and the counterweight still drains L slowly, and the page says so.
  • Springs that ring: damping and resonance: The resonant amplification 1/(2ζ) is the small-damping approximation for the peak.
  • Launching a specimen: projectile motion: The 1 % long fall is the planck.js semi-implicit Euler error at 120 Hz, measured in a test drop (0.8 % over 1 s).
  • Materials and densities: Density ranges for brass, cast iron and oak are given as ranges (alloy, grade, species and moisture).
  • Energy: kept, and lost: Joule's value is rounded to "about 4.2 J per gram per degree" (modern 4.18 J/(g·K)).
  • The Leyden jar and the Wimshurst machine: The Wimshurst machine is dated "early 1880s" (developed c. 1880–1883) and flagged as later than the notebook's 1873 date.
  • How the simulation works: Tunnelling is stated as "could in principle" and limited to moving objects: Box2D-style continuous collision protects against static bodies, and the game marks no bodies as bullets.
  • The Leyden jar and the Wimshurst machine: The Wimshurst source is a secondary textbook (Thompson), not Wimshurst's own paper, whose exact reference we could not confirm.

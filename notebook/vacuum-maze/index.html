Tilting the apparatus

A phone's accelerometer feels gravity. When the screen is tipped by an angle θ from flat, the part of gravity that lies along the screen is g·sin θ; that is the pull the atom feels in the game.

Lying flat (θ = 0) there is no pull at all; standing upright (θ = 90°) the atom gets the full g.

Analogy

Like a marble on a tea tray: tip the tray and the marble rolls downhill, more steeply the more you tip it.

How the game differs

The real apparatus would not tip with your hand; the game imagines the whole maze lying on your screen.

cf. W3C, DeviceOrientation Event Specification (devicemotion, accelerationIncludingGravity).

Gravity on Earth, Mars and the Moon

Surface gravity is 9.81 m/s² on Earth, 3.71 m/s² on Mars and 1.62 m/s² on the Moon.

With less gravity a falling atom picks up speed more slowly, so the same maze takes longer, and every tilt matters less.

How the game differs

"Arcade" mode lets you pick any gravity you like; times there are kept separately and earn fewer coins.

cf. NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Planetary Fact Sheets (Earth, Mars, Moon).

Knudsen's cosine law

A gas molecule that hits a real (rough, dirty) surface does not bounce like a billiard ball. It forgets where it came from and leaves in a random direction.

Directions near the surface normal are most likely: the chance falls off as the cosine of the angle θ from the normal. Martin Knudsen showed this experimentally in 1909–1915.

So a wall can just as easily send the atom back the way it came; that is why tilting matters so much.

Analogy

A wall lit by a lamp looks equally bright from every angle; light scattered from a matte surface follows the same cosine law (Lambert's law).

Simplification

The game is flat (2D) and draws each hit from the Cercignani–Lampis kernel (see "Walls trade energy"). Fully diffusing walls give exactly this cosine law; the default walls keep part of the incoming direction, so exits lean a little toward the mirror direction. Atoms arriving at the wall's own temperature still leave with the cosine law.

cf. M. Knudsen, Ann. Phys. 28, 75 (1909) and 48, 1113 (1915); F. Celestini & F. Mortessagne, Phys. Rev. E 77, 021202 (2008).

Walls trade energy with the atom

A hit on a wall is a small, random exchange of energy with the vibrating metal. Physicists describe it with a scattering kernel: the odds of every possible outgoing velocity, given the incoming one.

The game uses the Cercignani–Lampis kernel. Two accommodation coefficients, αₙ for the motion into the wall and σₜ for the motion along it, say how strongly a hit pulls the atom toward the wall: 0 is a perfect mirror, 1 forgets the incoming velocity completely.

With the default αₙ = σₜ = 0.5, the outgoing velocity is drawn around a point between the incoming velocity and the wall's thermal value, with a spread set by the wall temperature. A fast atom usually loses energy and a slow one usually gains, but either can go the other way. Over several hits the atom settles to the wall temperature.

Atoms that arrive with the wall's own temperature leave with it (detailed balance), so a wall never heats or cools a gas that is already at its temperature.

Analogy

Like a ball thrown at a lumpy, slightly soft wall: it comes back slower and in a somewhat random direction, but still remembers a little of how it was thrown.

Simplification

Real coefficients depend on the gas, the surface, its cleanliness and the impact energy. Light atoms on clean metals accommodate poorly (energy coefficients of a few hundredths for helium on clean tungsten); on ordinary, contaminated "engineering" surfaces values of roughly 0.3–0.9 are typical. 0.5 is a middle value: nobody has measured accommodation for microkelvin atoms.

How the game differs

Settings → Wall accommodation sets αₙ = σₜ, and you can switch to Maxwell's older model (each hit either a mirror bounce or fully diffuse). The game is flat, so only one direction along the wall is kept. The short stick to the wall before leaving is a pacing device; a real scattering event is effectively instantaneous.

cf. C. Cercignani & M. Lampis, Transp. Theory Stat. Phys. 1, 101 (1971); R. G. Lord, Phys. Fluids A 3, 706 (1991); F. O. Goodman, Prog. Surf. Sci. 5, 261 (1974); F. C. Saxena & S. C. Joshi, Thermal Accommodation and Adsorption Coefficients of Gases, Hemisphere (1989).

Why the walls are colder than a microkelvin

Real vacuum tubes sit at room temperature, 295 K. A hydrogen atom leaving such a wall moves at about 2.7 km/s (rms). Thrown straight up, it would coast about 370 km before Earth's gravity stopped it (about 395 km if you allow for gravity weakening with height), so tilting a tube by a few degrees could never steer it.

So the game makes its walls fantastically cold. Each level's wall temperature is T_w = κ·m·g·D / k_B: the energy of falling one bore diameter D, times a level factor κ (0.98–1.56). An atom leaving the wall then moves at a mean speed of √(8κgD/π), about 13% faster than √(2gD) at κ = 1: 0.7–2.3 m/s on Earth, 0.4–1.4 m/s on Mars and 0.3–0.9 m/s on the Moon. Gravity can bend its path within one tube width.

For hydrogen on Earth that gives 19 µK in a DN16CF tube and 176 µK in a DN160CF tube (at κ = 1); the shipped levels span 24–245 µK. On the Moon the walls are six times colder still. Heavier atoms get proportionally warmer walls: krypton in a DN63CF tube on Earth needs about 6 mK.

Analogy

Like playing marbles on a tilted table: the game slows everything down until a gentle tilt matters.

Simplification

No real wall is this cold. Dilution refrigerators reach a few millikelvin, and real cold-atom experiments keep their atoms in traps away from the 295 K walls: an atom that touches the wall is lost.

How the game differs

The cold-trap (77/295) and bake-out (443/295) ratios are applied to these microkelvin walls, not to 295 K.

cf. kB = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K (SI, exact); hydrogen mass 1.008 u (CIAAW); F. Pobell, Matter and Methods at Low Temperatures, Springer (2007) (dilution refrigerators).

Falling in a tube: parabolas between bounces

Between two wall hits nothing touches the atom except gravity, so it flies on an exact parabola: x = x₀ + vₓt, y = y₀ + v_yt + ½g∥t², where g∥ = g·sin(tilt) is the part of gravity along the screen.

Energy is shared between motion and height: ½mv² + mgh stays constant in flight. An atom leaving the floor upward at 1.25 m/s rises v²/2g = 8 cm on Earth (0.25 s up and down), 21 cm on Mars (0.67 s) and 48 cm on the Moon (1.5 s).

Thrown at the same speed, an atom goes six times higher on the Moon and takes six times longer. The game instead makes each world's walls colder in proportion to g, so the atom always rises about one bore, but every hop on the Moon takes √6 ≈ 2.5 times longer: that is why it feels floaty.

How the game differs

The game moves the atom along the exact parabola rather than an approximate step, in sub-steps short enough that it never travels more than 0.4 of its own radius, and checks for wall contact after each one. Energy in flight is conserved to rounding (about 10⁻¹⁴ in the tests) and atoms never tunnel through metal.

cf. Any mechanics text, e.g. D. Halliday, R. Resnick & J. Walker, Fundamentals of Physics, ch. 4 (projectile motion).

How much gets through a pipe: Clausing's transmission probability

In high vacuum molecules never meet each other, only the walls. A molecule that enters a tube bounces from wall to wall in random (cosine) directions, and most of them end up coming back out of the end they went in.

The chance of getting through is the transmission probability W, computed by Pieter Clausing in 1932. For a round tube: length = diameter, W ≈ 0.51; length = 5 diameters, W ≈ 0.19; length = 10 diameters, W ≈ 0.11; length = 25 diameters, W ≈ 0.048. Long tubes approach W ≈ 4d/3L.

The tube's conductance is W times that of a hole of the same size, about 11.6 L/s per cm² for air at 20 °C. For a long tube that gives C ≈ 12.1·d³/L L/s (d and L in cm): halve the diameter and the pipe passes eight times less.

Analogy

A pinball that has to get down a long corridor by bouncing off the walls at random: most of the time it bounces back out where it came in.

How the game differs

The maze is a flat (2D) version of this problem, with gravity added. With the tablet flat, the atom would wander like Clausing's molecules; tilting is how you beat the odds. The game's default walls also keep part of the incoming direction (see "Walls trade energy"), which helps atoms through.

cf. P. Clausing, Ann. Phys. 12, 961 (1932) (English: J. Vac. Sci. Technol. 8, 636 (1971)); M. Knudsen, Ann. Phys. 28, 75 (1909); Jousten (2016), ch. 4. Values above checked with a 200,000-molecule Monte Carlo.

Sticking for a moment

An atom that lands on a wall stays for a residence time before thermal vibrations shake it off. Frenkel's rule: τ = τ₀·e^(E/kT), with τ₀ about 10⁻¹³ s and E the binding energy.

Weakly bound atoms leave in well under a nanosecond; water stuck to steel can stay for hours, which is why vacuum systems are baked.

How the game differs

The game makes the wait about a tenth of a second so you can see it; for real it would be far too short to notice.

cf. J. Frenkel, Z. Phys. 26, 117 (1924); P. A. Redhead, Vacuum 12, 203 (1962).

Real sizes: ConFlat flanges

UHV tubing is joined with ConFlat (CF) flanges: a sharp knife-edge on each flange bites into a soft copper gasket, making an all-metal seal that survives baking to about 450 °C.

Sizes go by nominal diameter: DN16, DN40, DN63, DN100, DN160 have bores of roughly 16, 35, 60, 98 and 148 mm. Each level uses one size, shown on its card.

cf. W. R. Wheeler, Trans. Natl. Vac. Symp. 10, 159 (1963); ISO 3669:2017 (bakeable flanges).

Why a lone atom?

At 10⁻¹⁰ mbar an air molecule travels hundreds of kilometres between collisions with other molecules. Inside a pipe it only ever meets walls.

So in ultra-high vacuum every molecule really does make its own way through the pipes alone, bouncing from wall to wall: molecular flow. That is the world of this game.

Analogy

One moth in a long, empty corridor: it bumps into the walls again and again, but never into another moth.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 3–4: mean free path λ ≈ 6.6×10⁻³ cm·mbar / p for air at 20 °C.

Gate valves

A gate valve slides a flat plate (the gate) across the tube to seal one part of a system from another, for example a load-lock from the main chamber.

Open, it leaves the full bore clear; shut, it holds a vacuum on one side against air on the other.

How the game differs

Here the gates open and shut on a timer. Real ones are worked by hand, air pressure (pneumatic) or a motor, when the operator or a controller decides.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 18 (valves).

Apertures and differential pumping

A small hole between two chambers lets few molecules through: in molecular flow a thin aperture of area A passes gas at a rate C = v̄A/4 (about 11.6 litres per second per cm² for air at room temperature).

Put a pump on each side of a small hole and one side can be kept far emptier than the other. Molecular-beam machines and electron microscopes use chains of these stages.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 4 (conductance of an orifice in molecular flow).

Cold traps and cryoshrouds

A wall cooled by liquid nitrogen sits at 77 K (−196 °C). Water and many other vapours freeze onto it and stay there, so cold walls act as pumps.

MBE growth chambers are lined with liquid-nitrogen-cooled cryoshrouds for exactly this reason.

How the game differs

In the game the cold section sends the atom away colder, at 77/295 of that level's wall temperature (the ratio of liquid nitrogen to room temperature), so it slows down there. The game's walls are microkelvin-cold to begin with, so this is a fraction of a microkelvin, not 77 K (see "Why the walls are colder than a microkelvin").

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 16 (cryopumps); J. R. Arthur, Surf. Sci. 500, 189 (2002) (MBE).

Bake-out

To reach ultra-high vacuum, steel systems are wrapped in heater tape or put in an oven and baked, typically at 150–250 °C for a day or more, while pumping.

Heat shortens the residence time of water on the walls from hours to seconds, so it can be pumped away instead of slowly leaking off for weeks.

How the game differs

The game's heater tape sends the atom off hotter, at 443/295 of that level's wall temperature (the ratio of a 170 °C bake to room temperature): a kick. It is still only microkelvin, not 170 °C (see "Why the walls are colder than a microkelvin").

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 21; P. A. Redhead, J. Vac. Sci. Technol. A 13, 467 (1995).

Pushing atoms with light

Each photon carries momentum h/λ. When an atom absorbs one from a laser beam it gets a kick along the beam; when it re-emits, the kick goes in a random direction and averages away.

Tuned to the atom's resonance, an atom can scatter millions of photons a second, at most Γ/2 (half its natural decay rate). For sodium that is a push of about 10⁵ times gravity.

A beam only grips the atoms it is tuned to: sodium light does nothing to hydrogen. Laser colours in the game are the real wavelengths.

Simplification

Atoms are treated as two-level systems. Real atoms have hyperfine levels and need extra "repump" light to keep scattering.

How the game differs

The beams in the game are very weak (tens of nanowatts to a few microwatts per cm², about 3×10⁻⁸ to 6×10⁻⁴ of saturation) and tuned one linewidth below resonance. The push is 0.6–3 times gravity on an atom at rest, and at most 3–15 times gravity on one moving toward the beam at the Doppler-matched speed, not 10⁵. The Doppler-matched speeds are 2.1–22 m/s (Γ/k for these lines), while atoms in the game move at about 0.3–2.3 m/s, so in play the push stays close to the at-rest figure.

cf. H. J. Metcalf & P. van der Straten, Laser Cooling and Trapping, Springer (1999), ch. 3; C. J. Foot, Atomic Physics, OUP (2005), ch. 9; D. A. Steck, Sodium D Line Data (steck.us/alkalidata).

Saturation and how hard light can push

An atom scatters photons at the rate R = (Γ/2) · s / (1 + s + 4Δ²/Γ²), where s = I/I_sat is the intensity in units of the saturation intensity, Γ the natural decay rate and Δ the detuning (including the Doppler shift).

Turning up the light helps only until s ≈ 1. After that the atom spends half its time in the excited state and R levels off at Γ/2. For sodium (589 nm): I_sat = 6.26 mW/cm², Γ/2 = 3.1 × 10⁷ photons per second, a maximum push of ħkΓ/2m ≈ 9 × 10⁴ g.

A laser beam is brightest on its axis and fades as a Gaussian: s(ρ) = s₀·e^(−2ρ²/w²). At ρ = w (the "1/e² radius") the intensity is down to 13.5%.

How the game differs

Every beam in the game uses this formula with Δ = −Γ (one linewidth red of resonance) and a Gaussian profile with w a little over a quarter of the bore. With s₀ between about 3×10⁻⁸ and 6×10⁻⁴, the beams are far below saturation, so the push grows in proportion to the intensity.

cf. Metcalf & van der Straten (1999), ch. 2–3; Foot (2005), ch. 7 and 9; D. A. Steck, Sodium D Line Data (steck.us/alkalidata); A. E. Siegman, Lasers, University Science Books (1986), ch. 17 (Gaussian beams).

One photon at a time

Each absorbed photon changes the atom's velocity by the recoil velocity v_rec = h/(mλ). Light atoms and short wavelengths get the biggest kicks.

Sodium at 589 nm: 2.9 cm/s per photon. Lithium-7 at 671 nm: 8.5 cm/s (lithium-6: 9.9 cm/s; the game uses the natural average mass, 6.94 u, which gives 8.6). Potassium at 767 nm: 1.3 cm/s. Hydrogen at Lyman-α (121.6 nm): 3.3 m/s, more than its whole speed in the game.

So sodium needs about a thousand photons a second to feel a push of 3 g and glides smoothly, while hydrogen gets the same push from about nine photons a second, each one a visible jerk.

How the game differs

The game counts real, whole photons (a random Poisson number each step) and gives each re-emitted photon a random direction, so hydrogen in a Lyman-α beam really does lurch.

cf. Metcalf & van der Straten (1999), ch. 1; NIST Atomic Spectra Database v5.11 (wavelengths); h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s (SI, exact).

Doppler shift and detuning

An atom moving toward a laser sees its light shifted to a higher frequency by k·v (the Doppler shift). If the laser is tuned slightly below resonance ("red-detuned"), atoms moving toward it come into resonance and scatter more, so they are slowed.

This is the idea behind laser cooling, proposed by Hänsch and Schawlow and by Wineland and Dehmelt in 1975.

cf. T. W. Hänsch & A. L. Schawlow, Opt. Commun. 13, 68 (1975); D. J. Wineland & H. Dehmelt, Bull. Am. Phys. Soc. 20, 637 (1975).

Lyman-α: light for hydrogen

Hydrogen's strongest line, Lyman-α, is at 121.6 nm, deep in the vacuum ultraviolet: invisible, and absorbed by air, so it only travels in vacuum and through MgF₂ windows.

In 2021 the ALPHA experiment at CERN laser-cooled antihydrogen with Lyman-α light.

How the game differs

Invisible beams are drawn as dashed outlines.

cf. NIST Atomic Spectra Database v5.11; C. J. Baker et al. (ALPHA), Nature 592, 35 (2021).

Choosing an element

At the same temperature, heavier atoms are slower: speed goes as √(T/m).

Heavier does not mean bigger. An atom's size is set by its outer electrons, not its mass: potassium (39 u, van der Waals radius about 2.75 Å) is much larger than krypton (84 u, about 2.0 Å).

Each element has its own resonance lines, so a laser that pushes sodium leaves potassium alone. Pick the atom the lasers will not catch, or the one they will help.

How the game differs

The game sets each element's wall temperature in proportion to its mass, so every element moves at similar speeds and all of them are playable. It also draws heavier atoms bigger (radius 12 + 3√Z px) so you can tell them apart; that is a drawing choice, not real atomic size.

cf. NIST Atomic Spectra Database v5.11; atomic masses: CIAAW 2021; van der Waals radii: A. Bondi, J. Phys. Chem. 68, 441 (1964).

Margin note: see this for yourself in Vacuum Maze.

Rotary-vane pump

An off-centre rotor turns inside a cylinder. Vanes in slots slide out against the wall, trapping gas, squeezing it, and pushing it out through an oil-covered exhaust valve.

Oil seals the gaps and cools the pump. A two-stage pump reaches about 10⁻³ mbar from atmosphere: a roughing pump.

Analogy

A revolving door with sliding panels: each turn carries a pocket of air from one side to the other.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 7 (oil-sealed rotary pumps).

Scroll pump

Two interleaved spirals: one fixed, one orbiting without spinning. Crescent-shaped pockets of gas form at the rim and are squeezed toward the centre, where they are exhausted.

No oil touches the gas, so it is a clean "dry" roughing pump, typically reaching around 10⁻² mbar.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 7 (dry pumps).

Turbomolecular pump

Rows of angled blades spin at 24,000–90,000 rpm, so the blade tips move at a few hundred metres a second, as fast as the molecules themselves.

A molecule hitting a moving blade is more likely to be knocked down toward the outlet than back up. Rows of fixed blades in between guide it on. A backing pump removes the gas at the bottom.

Analogy

Like a tennis player swatting balls: whatever direction they arrive from, most get sent the same way.

cf. W. Becker, Vakuum-Technik 7, 149 (1958); Jousten (2016), ch. 12.

Sputter-ion pump

Rows of open steel tubes (anodes) sit between two titanium plates in a strong magnetic field (about 0.1–0.2 T) with several kilovolts across them: Penning cells.

Trapped electrons spiral round and ionise any gas molecule; the ion slams into the titanium, burying itself and sputtering fresh titanium that grabs more gas. No moving parts; it works down to about 10⁻¹¹ mbar.

Simplification

Noble gases are pumped only slowly by the simple (diode) type; "noble diode" and triode designs fix this.

cf. L. D. Hall, Rev. Sci. Instrum. 29, 367 (1958); Jousten (2016), ch. 15.

Titanium sublimation pump

A titanium-alloy filament is heated by a large current until titanium evaporates and coats the nearby walls.

The fresh film chemically binds active gases such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, CO and water. It cannot pump noble gases or methane, so it is usually paired with an ion pump.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 15 (getter pumps).

Non-evaporable getter (NEG)

A porous alloy such as Zr–V–Fe is heated (typically to about 400–450 °C) to "activate" it: the surface oxide dissolves into the bulk, leaving a clean, reactive metal surface.

Back at room temperature it soaks up hydrogen and binds CO, N₂, O₂ and water, with no power and no moving parts. CERN coats whole beam pipes with a related thin film.

cf. C. Benvenuti et al., Vacuum 53, 219 (1999); SAES Getters, St 707 data sheet.

Cryopump

A two-stage refrigerator (usually Gifford–McMahon) chills surfaces to about 50–80 K and 10–20 K. Water freezes on the warmer stage; nitrogen, oxygen and argon on the colder one.

Activated charcoal on the cold stage traps hydrogen, helium and neon, which would not freeze. The pump fills up and must be warmed now and then to release what it has caught (regeneration).

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 16; W. E. Gifford & H. O. McMahon, Adv. Cryog. Eng. 5, 354 (1960).

Diffusion pump

Oil is boiled at the bottom; its vapour rises up a central chimney and shoots downward from umbrella-shaped jets. Gas molecules that wander into the jets are carried down toward the outlet.

The oil condenses on water-cooled walls and runs back to the boiler. A backing pump is needed, and a cold trap above stops oil creeping into the chamber.

cf. W. Gaede, Ann. Phys. 46, 357 (1915); I. Langmuir, Phys. Rev. 8, 48 (1916); Jousten (2016), ch. 13.

Sorption pump

A canister of zeolite (a "molecular sieve" with huge internal surface) is dipped in liquid nitrogen. At 77 K it adsorbs most of the air in a chamber, with no oil and no moving parts.

It handles helium, neon and hydrogen poorly. Heating the zeolite afterwards releases the gas so it can be used again.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 16 (adsorption pumps).

Roots blower

Two figure-8 lobes on parallel shafts turn in opposite directions, kept in step by gears outside the pumping chamber so they never touch. Each turn carries pockets of gas round the outside.

It moves large volumes quickly at medium vacuum (about 10⁻³–10 mbar) but needs a backing pump behind it.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 9 (Roots pumps).

Holweck drag pump

A fast-spinning drum sits in a housing cut with helical grooves. Molecules that hit the moving surface pick up its sideways motion and are dragged along the grooves to the outlet.

Fernand Holweck built it in 1923. Today drag stages sit under the blades of many "turbo-drag" pumps so they can exhaust into a higher backing pressure.

cf. F. Holweck, C. R. Acad. Sci. 177, 43 (1923); Jousten (2016), ch. 12.

Ion gauge

A hot filament boils off electrons, which are pulled toward a wire-spiral grid. On the way they ionise gas molecules; a thin collector wire gathers the ions, and the ion current tells the pressure.

The Bayard–Alpert design (1950) reads down to about 10⁻¹¹ mbar. Its reading depends on the gas, so it is calibrated for nitrogen.

cf. R. T. Bayard & D. Alpert, Rev. Sci. Instrum. 21, 571 (1950).

Pirani gauge

A thin heated wire loses heat to the gas around it. Fewer molecules means less cooling, so the power needed to keep the wire hot tells the pressure.

Marcello Pirani invented it in 1906. It works for rough vacuum, roughly 10⁻³ mbar up to atmosphere in modern versions, and its reading depends on the gas.

cf. M. Pirani, Verh. Dtsch. Phys. Ges. 8, 686 (1906).

Capacitance manometer

A thin diaphragm bends as the pressure on one side changes. It forms one plate of a capacitor; measuring the capacitance measures the bending.

Because it responds to force, not to what the gas is, its reading does not depend on the type of gas. Each head covers about four decades of pressure.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 13 (diaphragm gauges).

Viewport

A window of glass, fused silica or sapphire sealed into a CF flange, so you can look in, or shine lasers in.

Different window materials pass different colours: ordinary glass blocks deep UV; magnesium fluoride lets even 121.6 nm Lyman-α light through.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 17 (feedthroughs and viewports).

Blank flange

A solid CF flange closes a port that is not in use. Its knife-edge bites a copper gasket just like any other CF joint.

Spare blanked ports are common: they leave room to add an instrument later.

cf. ISO 3669:2017.

Bellows

A flexible tube of welded or formed metal convolutions. It lets parts move, absorbs vibration, or takes up misalignment, while staying vacuum-tight.

Here it is blanked off at the far end.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 17.

All-metal valve

An angle valve seals with metal on metal (often a copper or hardened-steel seat) so it can be baked with the rest of the system.

Shut, it makes a dead end; open, it might lead to a pump-out port or a gas line.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 18.

Accuracy notes

Every entry was checked against the sources cited on its page. These claims are the ones we are least sure of, or that vary from one source or instrument to another:

  • Walls trade energy with the atom: The default α_n = σ_t = 0.5 is a judgement call. Measured values span roughly 0.02 (He on clean W) to ~0.9 (heavier gases on rough, contaminated metal); the "few hundredths" and "0.3–0.9" ranges are quoted from secondary summaries (Goodman 1974; Saxena & Joshi 1989) and were not checked against the original tables. No data exist at microkelvin impact energies, where quantum reflection and sticking would dominate.
  • Turbomolecular pump: Rotor speed range 24,000–90,000 rpm spans common commercial pumps; some small pumps exceed 90k rpm.
  • Sputter-ion pump: Field "about 0.1–0.2 T" and "down to about 10⁻¹¹ mbar" are typical catalogue values, not limits.
  • Non-evaporable getter (NEG): Activation "about 400–450 °C" is for St 707-type getters with short activation; lower temperatures work over longer times.
  • Scroll pump: Ultimate pressure "around 10⁻² mbar" varies by model (some reach a few 10⁻³ mbar).
  • Sorption pump: No ultimate pressure is given on purpose: it depends strongly on staging and on how much neon/helium is present.
  • Pirani gauge: Upper range "up to atmosphere in modern versions" refers to convection-enhanced Pirani gauges.
  • Bake-out: "150–250 °C for a day or more" is a typical stainless UHV bake; practice varies (LIGO's beam tubes were baked at roughly 150–170 °C for about a month; figure from secondary sources).
  • Choosing an element: Van der Waals radii depend on how they are defined and measured; Bondi (1964) gives K 2.75 Å and Kr 2.02 Å. Other tables differ by 10–20%, but potassium is larger than krypton in all of them.
  • lasers-li: Lithium line data (671 nm, Γ/2π = 5.87 MHz) are from Gehm's ⁶Li compilation. ⁷Li's D₂ line is shifted by roughly 10 GHz (about 0.015 nm) and has essentially the same linewidth; the shift is from secondary sources and was not checked against the original measurement. The game's lithium uses the natural-average mass (6.94 u), so its recoil (8.57 cm/s) sits between ⁷Li (8.48) and ⁶Li (9.89).
  • citations: Page numbers for Holweck (C. R. Acad. Sci. 1923) and Wheeler (CF flange, 1963 symposium) are from secondary references and should be checked against the originals.
  • levels: Some level captions summarise practice without a single primary source (e.g. load-lock pump-down times, diffusion pumps on large space-simulation chambers, lunar GW detector proposals).

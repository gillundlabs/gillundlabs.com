Tilting the apparatus

A phone's accelerometer feels gravity. When the screen is tipped by an angle θ from flat, the part of gravity that lies along the screen is g·sin θ.

That is the pull the atom feels: g∥ = g·sin θ, with the phone's tilt equal to the chamber's. Standing the phone upright (θ = 90°) gives the full 9.81 m/s² on Earth; lying flat (θ = 0) there is no pull at all. Start with the phone near level and the acceleration is gentle, as in a physical tilt maze; tip further and the pull grows with sin θ.

Simplification

The game is flat (2D): it keeps only the pull along the chamber and ignores gravity straight down into the screen. At the game's few nanokelvin a real chamber under full g would hold a hydrogen atom within a few micrometres of the tube floor (its gravitational scale height k_B·T/m·g is only 2–7 µm in this edition's levels), hopping a few micrometres at a time instead of flying across the tube at several mm/s.

How the game differs

The real apparatus would not tip with your hand; the game imagines the whole maze lying on your screen. Wall and start temperatures, not a scaled tilt, keep the atom at about a quarter of Maxwell's Demon's pace (see "Why the walls are colder than a microkelvin").

cf. W3C, DeviceOrientation Event Specification (devicemotion, accelerationIncludingGravity).

Gravity on Earth, Mars and the Moon

Surface gravity is 9.81 m/s² on Earth, 3.71 m/s² on Mars and 1.62 m/s² on the Moon.

With less gravity a falling atom picks up speed more slowly, so the same maze takes longer, and every tilt matters less.

How the game differs

"Arcade" mode lets you pick any gravity you like; times there are kept separately and earn fewer coins.

cf. NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Planetary Fact Sheets (Earth, Mars, Moon).

Knudsen's cosine law

A gas molecule that hits a real (rough, dirty) surface does not bounce like a billiard ball. It forgets where it came from and leaves in a random direction.

Directions near the surface normal are most likely: the chance falls off as the cosine of the angle θ from the normal. Martin Knudsen showed this experimentally in 1909–1915.

So a wall can just as easily send the atom back the way it came; that is why tilting matters so much.

Simplification

The game is flat (2D) and draws each hit from the Cercignani–Lampis kernel (see "Walls trade energy"). Fully diffusing walls give exactly this cosine law; the default walls keep part of the incoming direction, so exits lean a little toward the mirror direction. Atoms arriving at the wall's own temperature still leave with the cosine law.

cf. M. Knudsen, Ann. Phys. 28, 75 (1909) and 48, 1113 (1915); F. Celestini & F. Mortessagne, Phys. Rev. E 77, 021202 (2008).

Walls trade energy with the atom

A hit on a wall is a small, random exchange of energy with the vibrating metal. Physicists describe it with a scattering kernel: the odds of every possible outgoing velocity, given the incoming one.

The game uses the Cercignani–Lampis kernel. Two accommodation coefficients, αₙ for the motion into the wall and σₜ for the motion along it, say how strongly a hit pulls the atom toward the wall: 0 is a perfect mirror, 1 forgets the incoming velocity completely.

With the default αₙ = σₜ = 0.5, the outgoing velocity is drawn around a point between the incoming velocity and the wall's thermal value, with a spread set by the wall temperature. A fast atom usually loses energy and a slow one usually gains, but either can go the other way. Over several hits the atom settles to the wall temperature.

Atoms that arrive with the wall's own temperature leave with it (detailed balance), so a wall never heats or cools a gas that is already at its temperature.

Simplification

Real coefficients depend on the gas, the surface, its cleanliness and the impact energy. Light atoms on clean metals accommodate poorly (energy coefficients of a few hundredths for helium on clean tungsten); on ordinary, contaminated "engineering" surfaces values of roughly 0.3–0.9 are typical. 0.5 is a middle value: nobody has measured accommodation for micro- or nanokelvin atoms.

How the game differs

Settings → Wall accommodation sets αₙ = σₜ, and you can switch to Maxwell's older model (each hit either a mirror bounce or fully diffuse). The game is flat, so only one direction along the wall is kept. The short stick to the wall before leaving is a pacing device; a real scattering event is effectively instantaneous.

cf. C. Cercignani & M. Lampis, Transp. Theory Stat. Phys. 1, 101 (1971); R. G. Lord, Phys. Fluids A 3, 706 (1991); F. O. Goodman, Prog. Surf. Sci. 5, 261 (1974); F. C. Saxena & S. C. Joshi, Thermal Accommodation and Adsorption Coefficients of Gases, Hemisphere (1989).

Why the walls are colder than a microkelvin

Real vacuum tubes sit at room temperature, 295 K. A hydrogen atom leaving such a wall moves at about 2.7 km/s (rms). Thrown straight up, it would coast about 370 km before Earth's gravity stopped it (about 395 km if you allow for gravity weakening with height), so tilting a tube by a few degrees could never steer it.

So the game makes its walls fantastically cold — about a sixteenth of the temperature at which Maxwell's Demon releases its atoms, so the atom moves at about a quarter of that game's pace. Each level's wall temperature is T_w = κ·m·g_pace·D / k_B, with g_pace = g / 16000: the energy of falling one bore diameter D under a reference pull of one sixteen-thousandth of the world's surface gravity, times a level factor κ (0.98–1.11 in this edition's 30 levels; up to 1.56 in later chapters). An atom leaving the wall then moves at a mean speed of √(8κg_pace D/π), about 13% faster than √(2 g_pace D) at κ = 1. In this edition's levels that is 7–13 mm/s on Earth, 5–8 mm/s on Mars and 3–5 mm/s on the Moon (6–18, 3–11 and 2–7 mm/s across all 100 planned levels), about a quarter of the 31 mm/s at which Maxwell's Demon releases its atoms. The game then scales that temperature with the width of maze on the screen, so the time an atom takes to cross the screen stays the same on every screen; on a large screen the temperature dial therefore reads higher than these few nanokelvin and the few millimetres per second. The chamber pull itself is not scaled: the atom feels the full g·sin θ (see "Tilting the apparatus").

For hydrogen on Earth that gives 1 nK in a DN16CF tube and 11 nK in a DN160CF tube (at κ = 1); this edition's 30 levels span 3–8 nK (2–15 nK across all 100 planned levels), about a sixteenth of the 40 nK at which Maxwell's Demon starts its hydrogen. On the Moon the walls are six times colder still. Heavier atoms get proportionally warmer walls: krypton in a DN63CF tube on Earth needs about 371 nK.

Simplification

No real wall is this cold. Dilution refrigerators reach a few millikelvin, and real cold-atom experiments keep their atoms in traps away from the 295 K walls: an atom that touches the wall is lost. Under full Earth gravity the same nanokelvin temperatures give a gravitational scale height of only tens of micrometres, so a real atom would sit on the tube floor; the game is flat and ignores that direction (see "Tilting the apparatus").

How the game differs

Cold traps and bake-out tape arrive with the free-update chapters (and become level-editor parts then); there, the real 77/295 and 443/295 temperature ratios are applied to these nanokelvin walls, not to 295 K.

cf. kB = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K (SI, exact); hydrogen mass 1.008 u (CIAAW); F. Pobell, Matter and Methods at Low Temperatures, Springer (2007) (dilution refrigerators).

Falling in a tube: parabolas between bounces

Between two wall hits nothing touches the atom except gravity, so it flies on an exact parabola: x = x₀ + vₓt, y = y₀ + v_yt + ½g∥t², where g∥ = g·sin θ is the part of gravity along the chamber for a phone tipped by θ (see "Tilting the apparatus").

Energy is shared between motion and height: ½mv² + mg∥h stays constant in flight. With the phone upright on Earth (g∥ = 9.81 m/s²), an atom leaving the floor upward at the wall's 9.9 mm/s rises only v²/2g∥ = 5 µm (2.0 ms up and down); at the matching wall speed on each world it rises the same 5 µm on Mars (3 ms) and the same 5 µm on the Moon (5 ms).

The walls are colder on weaker worlds in proportion to g (see "Why the walls are colder than a microkelvin"), so the hop height stays the same while every hop on the Moon takes √6 ≈ 2.5 times longer: that is why it feels floaty. Tip only a little and g∥ is small, so the same thermal speed reaches much higher — near level, a hop of about one bore.

How the game differs

The game moves the atom along the exact parabola rather than an approximate step, in sub-steps short enough that it never travels more than 0.4 of its own radius, and checks for wall contact after each one. Energy in flight is conserved to rounding (about 10⁻¹⁴ in the tests) and atoms never tunnel through metal. The game is flat: those micrometre hops are along the maze, not into the floor of a real tube (see "Tilting the apparatus").

cf. Any mechanics text, e.g. D. Halliday, R. Resnick & J. Walker, Fundamentals of Physics, ch. 4 (projectile motion).

How much gets through a pipe: Clausing's transmission probability

In high vacuum molecules never meet each other, only the walls. A molecule that enters a tube bounces from wall to wall in random (cosine) directions, and most of them end up coming back out of the end they went in.

The chance of getting through is the transmission probability W, computed by Pieter Clausing in 1932. For a round tube: length = diameter, W ≈ 0.51; length = 5 diameters, W ≈ 0.19; length = 10 diameters, W ≈ 0.11; length = 25 diameters, W ≈ 0.048. Long tubes approach W ≈ 4d/3L.

The tube's conductance is W times that of a hole of the same size, about 11.6 L/s per cm² for air at 20 °C. For a long tube that gives C ≈ 12.1·d³/L L/s (d and L in cm): halve the diameter and the pipe passes eight times less.

How the game differs

The maze is a flat (2D) version of this problem, with gravity added. With the tablet flat, the atom would wander like Clausing's molecules; tilting is how you beat the odds. The game's default walls also keep part of the incoming direction (see "Walls trade energy"), which helps atoms through.

cf. P. Clausing, Ann. Phys. 12, 961 (1932) (English: J. Vac. Sci. Technol. 8, 636 (1971)); M. Knudsen, Ann. Phys. 28, 75 (1909); Jousten (2016), ch. 4. Values above checked with a 200,000-molecule Monte Carlo.

Sticking for a moment

An atom that lands on a wall stays for a residence time before thermal vibrations shake it off. Frenkel's rule: τ = τ₀·e^(E/kT), with τ₀ about 10⁻¹³ s and E the binding energy.

Weakly bound atoms leave in well under a nanosecond; water stuck to steel can stay for hours, which is why vacuum systems are baked.

How the game differs

The game makes the wait about a tenth of a second so you can see it; for real it would be far too short to notice.

cf. J. Frenkel, Z. Phys. 26, 117 (1924); P. A. Redhead, Vacuum 12, 203 (1962).

Real sizes: ConFlat flanges

UHV tubing is joined with ConFlat (CF) flanges: a sharp knife-edge on each flange bites into a soft copper gasket, making an all-metal seal that survives baking to about 450 °C.

Sizes go by nominal diameter: DN16, DN40, DN63, DN100, DN160 have bores of roughly 16, 35, 60, 98 and 148 mm. Each level uses one size, shown on its card.

cf. W. R. Wheeler, Trans. Natl. Vac. Symp. 10, 159 (1963); ISO 3669:2017 (bakeable flanges).

Why a lone atom?

At 10⁻¹⁰ mbar an air molecule travels hundreds of kilometres between collisions with other molecules. Inside a pipe it only ever meets walls.

So in ultra-high vacuum every molecule really does make its own way through the pipes alone, bouncing from wall to wall: molecular flow. That is the world of this game.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 3–4: mean free path λ ≈ 6.6×10⁻³ cm·mbar / p for air at 20 °C.

Gate valves

A gate valve slides a flat plate (the gate) across the tube to seal one part of a system from another, for example a load-lock from the main chamber.

Open, it leaves the full bore clear; shut, it holds a vacuum on one side against air on the other.

How the game differs

Here the gates open and shut on a timer. Real ones are worked by hand, air pressure (pneumatic) or a motor, when the operator or a controller decides.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 18 (valves).

Square or arced elbows? Conductance round a bend

In high vacuum a molecule meets only the walls. Each time it hits one it sticks for an instant and leaves in a new random direction that does not depend on how it arrived. Knudsen's cosine law gives the odds: the flux leaving at an angle θ from the wall's normal is proportional to cos θ (not cos² θ), so straight out from the wall is the most likely direction. A fitting's conductance measures how readily it passes molecules from one end to the other.

Picture one molecule going round a 90° bend. In a square (mitred) elbow, the far wall of the incoming leg is a flat end wall that faces straight down the outgoing leg, so a molecule leaving that wall near its normal heads into the exit leg. In an arced (radius) elbow the outer wall curves, and its normal everywhere points across the bend toward the inner wall, so molecules leaving near the normal cross from wall to wall as they work their way round.

That picture follows only one bounce. The same cosine law sends molecules back the way they came just as readily: the square elbow's other flat end wall faces straight back up the incoming leg. Counting every bounce, the two shapes come out even. For the same bore in molecular flow, mitred and radius elbows have essentially the same conductance; the Kurt J. Lesker fittings notes put it as identical. A radius elbow does conduct better in continuum and transitional flow, at higher pressures, where the gas moves as a fluid and a smooth bend disturbs the flow less.

So why are mitred elbows everywhere in high-vacuum systems? Not for a higher conductance at the same bore. A mitred elbow is two straight tube ends cut at 45° and welded, so its legs can be short and it turns the corner in little more room than the flanges need. A bent tube needs a bend radius of a few tube diameters, so a radius elbow of the same bore is bigger. In a crowded apparatus the compact mitred elbow gives the gas a shorter path and takes up less space.

Simplification

The square-corner and arc picture is a way to see where the cosine law sends molecules after one wall hit; it is not a calculation. Conductance comes from counting every bounce (for example by Monte Carlo, as Davis did in 1960), and for elbows of the same bore that count gives essentially the same answer for both shapes. Real conductance also depends on the length of each leg, which this page does not compare.

How the game differs

The level editor offers both shapes, at the same price; the official mazes use arced elbows. Both use the same walls as the rest of the maze (the Cercignani–Lampis kernel, see "Walls trade energy"), not a pure cosine law, and the maze is flat with gravity along it. There is only ever one atom, so there is no gas flow or flow regime in the game: your tilt, not conductance, decides how fast it gets round a corner.

cf. M. Knudsen, Ann. Phys. 28, 75 (1909); Kurt J. Lesker Company, Fittings, adaptors & couplings: technical notes (lesker.com); D. H. Davis, J. Appl. Phys. 31, 1169 (1960); R. G. Livesey, in J. M. Lafferty (ed.), Foundations of Vacuum Science and Technology, Wiley (1998), ch. 2.

Choosing an element

At the same temperature, heavier atoms are slower: speed goes as √(T/m).

Heavier does not mean bigger. An atom's size is set by its outer electrons, not its mass: potassium (39 u, van der Waals radius about 2.75 Å) is much larger than krypton (84 u, about 2.0 Å).

Each element has its own resonance lines, so a laser that pushes sodium leaves potassium alone. When the laser chapters arrive as a free update, those lasers also unlock in the level editor; pick the atom the lasers will not catch, or the one they will help.

How the game differs

The game sets each element's wall temperature in proportion to its mass, so every element moves at similar speeds and all of them are playable. It also draws heavier atoms bigger (radius 12 + 3√Z px) so you can tell them apart; that is a drawing choice, not real atomic size.

cf. NIST Atomic Spectra Database v5.11; atomic masses: CIAAW 2021; van der Waals radii: A. Bondi, J. Phys. Chem. 68, 441 (1964).

Rotary-vane pump

An off-centre rotor turns inside a cylinder. Vanes in slots slide out against the wall, trapping gas, squeezing it, and pushing it out through an oil-covered exhaust valve.

Oil seals the gaps and cools the pump. A two-stage pump reaches about 10⁻³ mbar from atmosphere: a roughing pump.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 7 (oil-sealed rotary pumps).

Margin note: see this for yourself in Vacuum Maze.

Scroll pump

Two interleaved spirals: one fixed, one orbiting without spinning. Crescent-shaped pockets of gas form at the rim and are squeezed toward the centre, where they are exhausted.

No oil touches the gas, so it is a clean "dry" roughing pump, typically reaching around 10⁻² mbar.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 7 (dry pumps).

Turbomolecular pump

Rows of angled blades spin at 24,000–90,000 rpm, so the blade tips move at a few hundred metres a second, as fast as the molecules themselves.

A molecule hitting a moving blade is more likely to be knocked down toward the outlet than back up. Rows of fixed blades in between guide it on. A backing pump removes the gas at the bottom.

cf. W. Becker, Vakuum-Technik 7, 149 (1958); Jousten (2016), ch. 12.

Sputter-ion pump

Rows of open steel tubes (anodes) sit between two titanium plates in a strong magnetic field (about 0.1–0.2 T) with several kilovolts across them: Penning cells.

Trapped electrons spiral round and ionise any gas molecule; the ion slams into the titanium, burying itself and sputtering fresh titanium that grabs more gas. No moving parts; it works down to about 10⁻¹¹ mbar.

Simplification

Noble gases are pumped only slowly by the simple (diode) type; "noble diode" and triode designs fix this.

cf. L. D. Hall, Rev. Sci. Instrum. 29, 367 (1958); Jousten (2016), ch. 15.

Titanium sublimation pump

A titanium-alloy filament is heated by a large current until titanium evaporates and coats the nearby walls.

The fresh film chemically binds active gases such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, CO and water. It cannot pump noble gases or methane, so it is usually paired with an ion pump.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 15 (getter pumps).

Ion gauge

A hot filament boils off electrons, which are pulled toward a wire-spiral grid. On the way they ionise gas molecules; a thin collector wire gathers the ions, and the ion current tells the pressure.

The Bayard–Alpert design (1950) reads down to about 10⁻¹¹ mbar. Its reading depends on the gas, so it is calibrated for nitrogen.

cf. R. T. Bayard & D. Alpert, Rev. Sci. Instrum. 21, 571 (1950).

Pirani gauge

A thin heated wire loses heat to the gas around it. Fewer molecules means less cooling, so the power needed to keep the wire hot tells the pressure.

Marcello Pirani invented it in 1906. It works for rough vacuum, roughly 10⁻³ mbar up to atmosphere in modern versions, and its reading depends on the gas.

cf. M. Pirani, Verh. Dtsch. Phys. Ges. 8, 686 (1906).

Capacitance manometer

A thin diaphragm bends as the pressure on one side changes. It forms one plate of a capacitor; measuring the capacitance measures the bending.

Because it responds to force, not to what the gas is, its reading does not depend on the type of gas. Each head covers about four decades of pressure.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 13 (diaphragm gauges).

Viewport

A window of glass, fused silica or sapphire sealed into a CF flange, so you can look in, or shine lasers in.

Different window materials pass different colours: ordinary glass blocks deep UV; magnesium fluoride lets even 121.6 nm Lyman-α light through.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 17 (feedthroughs and viewports).

Blank flange

A solid CF flange closes a port that is not in use. Its knife-edge bites a copper gasket just like any other CF joint.

Spare blanked ports are common: they leave room to add an instrument later.

cf. ISO 3669:2017.

Bellows

A flexible tube of welded or formed metal convolutions. It lets parts move, absorbs vibration, or takes up misalignment, while staying vacuum-tight.

Here it is blanked off at the far end.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 17.

All-metal valve

An angle valve seals with metal on metal (often a copper or hardened-steel seat) so it can be baked with the rest of the system.

Shut, it makes a dead end; open, it might lead to a pump-out port or a gas line.

cf. Jousten (2016), ch. 18.

Accuracy notes

Every entry was checked against the sources cited on its page. These claims are the ones we are least sure of, or that vary from one source or instrument to another (notes for entries that arrive in the free updates come with them):

  • Square or arced elbows? Conductance round a bend: The "identical in molecular flow" wording is the Kurt J. Lesker fittings note; we did not reproduce a same-bore mitred-versus-radius Monte Carlo ourselves, so the page says "essentially the same". "A bend radius of a few tube diameters" for bent tube is typical of tube-bending practice and varies by maker and wall thickness. The square-corner and arc picture is a one-bounce illustration (labelled as such), not a result.
  • Walls trade energy with the atom: The default α_n = σ_t = 0.5 is a judgement call. Measured values span roughly 0.02 (He on clean W) to ~0.9 (heavier gases on rough, contaminated metal); the "few hundredths" and "0.3–0.9" ranges are quoted from secondary summaries (Goodman 1974; Saxena & Joshi 1989) and were not checked against the original tables. No data exist at micro- or nanokelvin impact energies, where quantum reflection and sticking would dominate.
  • Turbomolecular pump: Rotor speed range 24,000–90,000 rpm spans common commercial pumps; some small pumps exceed 90k rpm.
  • Sputter-ion pump: Field "about 0.1–0.2 T" and "down to about 10−11 mbar" are typical catalogue values, not limits.
  • Scroll pump: Ultimate pressure "around 10−2 mbar" varies by model (some reach a few 10−3 mbar).
  • Pirani gauge: Upper range "up to atmosphere in modern versions" refers to convection-enhanced Pirani gauges.
  • Choosing an element: Van der Waals radii depend on how they are defined and measured; Bondi (1964) gives K 2.75 Å and Kr 2.02 Å. Other tables differ by 10–20%, but potassium is larger than krypton in all of them.
  • Real sizes: ConFlat flanges: The page number for Wheeler (CF flange, 1963 symposium) is from a secondary reference and should be checked against the original.
  • Level captions: Some level captions summarise practice without a single primary source (e.g. load-lock pump-down times).
